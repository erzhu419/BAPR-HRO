                   Reviewer Report for
     Re-Ranking Stochastic Transit Hyperpaths Under
    Disruption: A Wasserstein-Robust Scoring Approach
                                     Operations Research



Summary
This paper studies transit routing under disruptions. The authors retain a precomputed stochastic
hyperpath and re-rank its candidate connections using a score based on Bayesian estimates of delays
and cancellations. They develop a Wasserstein DRO interpretation of this score’s posterior risk,
present a Lean formalization of the supporting analysis, and evaluate the approach against static
and adaptive routing baselines through simulation experiments.


Recommendation
I would like to thank the authors for an interesting paper. The studied subject is of interest to our
community. I also believe including Lean formalization of the proofs is a great initiative. I wish
more papers would adopt this approach. However, the paper has multiple significant shortcomings
that cannot be addressed in a reasonable review period. Even if the shortcomings were addressed,
I believe the novelty does not meet the high bar of Operations Research. I would like to therefore
recommend a rejection. Some of my concerns are enumerated below.


Concerns
  1. I believe there are several mistakes in the theorem statements and proofs. For example, the
     condition stated as necessary and sufficient in Theorem 3(b) is sufficient but not necessary:
     for 0 ≤ γ < 1, the robust Bellman operator is γ-contractive for all ε ≥ 0. Moreover, its proof
     has a mistake in the inequality ΦεW1 (V ) ≥ EP̂ [V ] + ε · inf{V }. To see this, take V as a positive
     constant and fix any ε > 0. The stated inequality implies 0 ≥ ε · V > 0.
  2. The Wasserstein DRO equivalence of the scoring function is not surprising and should not be
     presented as a key methodological contribution. For the loss function studied in this paper,
     any score consisting of the nominal expected cost plus a nonnegative penalty admits this
     Wasserstein DRO interpretation by choosing the ambiguity radius ε equal to that penalty
     (including trivial/meaningless penalty functions).
  3. The radius ε lacks a statistical calibration. As discussed in the previous item, it is set equal
     to the chosen score penalty, without establishing that the resulting ambiguity set contains the
     true (unknown) distribution with a prescribed confidence level. Consequently, the equivalence
     alone does not provide an out-of-sample guarantee with high probability. It only recasts the
     proposed score as a DRO objective.
  4. The presentation is difficult to follow. There are many small independent subsections that are
     not linked well. The order of the sections is also somewhat confusing: for example, Section 5.2
     introduces the Wasserstein DRO equivalence, which is revisited again in Section 6.3.

  5. There is a strong emphasis on introducing Lean formalization. An Operations Research paper
     is already expected to have correct proofs, hence Lean code can only be valuable supplementary
     material. However, the paper discusses details of the Lean code in many places. This distracts
     the reader from the main transportation problem. This emphasis also gives attention to
     well-known results, including the pseudometric properties of W1 and the weak Kantorovich–
     Rubinstein inequality, which are standard background results rather than new contributions
     specific to the proposed model.
  6. The Bayesian modeling choices appear to be motivated primarily by computational convenience
     due to conjugacy. There is insufficient justification of their suitability for the transit domain
     studied in this paper. The connection between the resulting posterior uncertainty and the
     DRO formulation also needs a clearer statistical interpretation.
  7. There is too much going on at once in the presentation, and the role of the different frame-
     works and technical details is often not clear. Several sections move directly into a collection
     of subsections without first explaining the section’s purpose or how its components fit to-
     gether. Some subsections consist of only two sentences, further fragmenting the discussion and
     making the progression of the argument difficult to follow. The manuscript moves rapidly be-
     tween stochastic hyperpaths, Bayesian inference, DRO, MDPs, online learning, computational
     complexity, formal verification, and neural surrogates. It is often unclear which ingredients
     define the proposed method, which provide an interpretation of it, which concern competing
     approaches, and which are ancillary extensions. The breadth of fields invoked also makes
     it difficult to identify the substantive contribution made within or between them. Technical
     terminology and abbreviations accumulate before the central argument has been adequately
     developed: planning trees, “Stuttgart-style” algorithms, PSPACE-hardness, EXP3, sliding-
     window methods, pessimism and optimism, VaR/CVaR, among others. Terms such as “SBB”
     and “cell-mean” also need explanation on first use. Meanwhile, details of other papers, such
     as the number of forward simulations used by a competing method, interrupt the exposition
     without making their relevance clear. A more focused narrative would help establish the deci-
     sion problem, explain the proposed approach, and identify its novelty before introducing these
     frameworks and secondary details.
  8. The manuscript only cites foundational papers in (D)RO and robust MDPs. Several relevant
     papers are not cited. Some examples: Yang (2021), Wiesemann et al. (2013), Shapiro et al.
     (2023), Wozabal (2014).
